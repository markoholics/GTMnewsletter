A long read on why the most valuable column in your signal list is the one almost nobody fills in: how long the buyer's window stays open.

By Mohammad H. Rahman

Most go to market teams are buying signals faster than they can answer them. Somewhere in your stack there is a feed of job changes, funding rounds, pricing page visits and usage spikes, and somewhere in your week there is a rep who looks at it on Thursday. The gap between those two moments is the most expensive number you do not track.

Five of this week's GTM newsletters, written for quite different readers, circled that gap without naming it. Each described a buyer whose situation changed, a window that opened, and a vendor who arrived on time or late. None of them put the three side by side. This is a long read because the idea only works once you have seen the cases together.

A cancellation that was public by the next morning

Doug Bell, who writes Cannonball GTM, is building a method for finding the pains in a market that can put a buyer out of business, using nothing but public data. His running example is a US trucking company. When its insurer cancels the policy, the government posts the cancellation in a free file the next morning. The owner finds out ten days later, when a broker will not give him a load, and by then ten of his 33 days to fix the problem are gone (per Cannonball GTM). Bell's verdict on the software category around that owner: every vendor could have seen it, and nobody called.

[Image 2: insight-card.png. Upload from the images folder. Alt text: Large 33 days: the time a trucking company has to fix a cancelled insurance policy, ten already gone when the owner hears, per Cannonball GTM]

Two details matter more than the drama. The pain has a line, set by someone with authority, and it has a clock. Bell's bar for putting a pain on his map is four yes or no questions: can you count it, is there a line, is it public, could it shut the business down (per Cannonball GTM). Of twelve trucking pains he tested, seven passed, and three of those run on a countdown: 33 days after a cancellation, 45 days for a rating review, and 61 days after a proposed failing rating (per Cannonball GTM). What survives the bar he sums up in three words (per Cannonball GTM).

"Public. Dated. Deadly." Doug Bell, Cannonball GTM

[Image 3: quote-card.png. Upload from the images folder. Alt text: Quote card: Public. Dated. Deadly., attributed to Doug Bell of Cannonball GTM]

He also attaches numbers to the commercial result. Generic cold outreach books meetings at 0.02 per cent, pain based outreach at 1 per cent, and pain based outreach with a strong message at 4 per cent or better (per Cannonball GTM). I would treat those as the author's working figures, since the post does not show how they were measured. The direction is still plausible. A message that arrives with a reason and a date behaves differently from one that arrives with a category.

Twenty five hours is a clock too

Now the same idea from inside a company. Alex Lindahl, who writes the GTM Engineering newsletter for Clay, reports that in early 2025 an inbound lead at monday.com waited about 25 hours on average to hear from a human. By the time Oran Akron left the company this month, the wait was under two minutes, with an AI voice agent calling the lead, qualifying them and booking a rep while the prospect was still on the phone (per the GTM Engineering newsletter). Akron's reasoning was that fixing speed to lead would lift conversion at every stage below it (per the GTM Engineering newsletter). That is a belief rather than a finding, and the figures are reported rather than audited.

Whoever said good things come to those who wait clearly never watched an inbound lead go cold in a queue for 25 hours. A form fill is about the strongest signal a buyer can send. Everything about their situation is fresh on the day they send it, and a market with alternatives gives them plenty of other people to talk to by the next morning.

Signals that live inside the product

The third case is quieter. Marcus Holm, the chief revenue officer at Exa, told the GTMnow podcast that his reps read adoption patterns and tell customers what they have noticed, then suggest something to try next (per GTMnow). A customer in the middle of an experiment is paying attention now and may not be next week, so this is a signal with a very short fuse. It needs no contract date and no campaign calendar.

The large vendors are buying the plumbing for it. Salesforce has agreed to acquire Braze for about $5.66 billion (per GTMnow and per GTM Strategist), and GTMnow reads the deal as the clearest sign yet that CRM giants want event driven engagement rather than scheduled campaigns and static segments (per GTMnow). The Signal, a newsletter on GTM engineering, draws the same line from the job side: RevOps keeps the system of record, and GTM engineers own the system of action (per The Signal). Five newsletters, then, describe the same shift from calendars to events.

The column your signal list is missing

None of those writers makes the point I want to make, which is that each treats the signal as the asset. The public file, the form fill, the usage spike and the event stream are the things being bought, built and celebrated. Lindahl's advice on spending shows the mood well: run an enrichment only if something is true, for instance look up a mobile number only when the lead fits your ideal customer (per the GTM Engineering newsletter). Sensible about cost, and silent about time.

Ask what a signal is worth and the usual answer is a score. But does a score say whether the buyer is still in the market on Friday? A cancelled policy gives a trucker 33 days, and a form fill gives you, on the evidence of monday's queue, a good deal less. Does your list say which is which? A job change posted three weeks ago and a pricing page visit from this morning sit in the same row of most CRMs, carrying roughly the same weight. And what happens to the signals nobody answers inside their window? They go on feeding your dashboards, quietly, as evidence that signals do not work.

The other side has a fair case, and I would concede three parts of it. Reaching a buyer late does not always lose the deal, and plenty of revenue closes on patience. Brand does work no stopwatch can see: Aviv Canaani, the chief revenue officer at Datarails, told GTMnow that a prospect on an outbound call said "I see your ads all the time" (per GTMnow), a reminder that part of any reply was earned months before. And speed will not rescue a poor message. The Cannonball figures above separate 0.02 per cent from 4 per cent on reason and message, with no mention of speed. GTMnow also reported Seismic research in which 75 per cent of 200 B2B leaders said they trust human guidance over AI output when the two conflict (per GTMnow), so a fast machine may simply reach the wrong conversation sooner.

All of that stands, and it still leaves the decision where it was. A signal is worth the window it opens, less the time you take to answer. Subtract the second from the first and a candid look at most lists will show signals that come out at zero or below.

Four questions for every signal

Before a signal earns budget, I would put four questions to it. The first borrows Bell's idea of a line.

Threshold. The level at which the signal changes the buyer's situation. An insurer's cancellation is a threshold. "Visited the website" usually is not.

Window. How many days or hours remain before the situation resolves, with or without you. The trucker's 33 days is a window. A job change has one as well, and it is shorter than most sequences.

Reflex. The measured time between the signal appearing and a human quality touch. monday's 25 hours was a reflex. If nobody has measured yours, your reflex is unknown, and unknown is the honest entry.

Owner. The named person who is alerted, with the authority to act without asking anyone.

[Image 4: framework.png. Upload from the images folder. Alt text: Four boxes in order: Threshold, Window, Reflex and Owner, each with the question to ask]

Here is a rule of thumb of mine, and it is not a finding from any source: keep a signal only if its window is at least five times your reflex. Hone the number to your own market, but keep some number.

What it looks like in a small team

A hypothetical, invented for illustration. Imagine a payroll software company with four salespeople and three signals on its dashboard: funding announcements, new HR hires and demo requests. Funding news has a window of months and a reflex of three days, so it passes comfortably, though it is hardly urgent. The HR hire has a window of weeks and a reflex of two days, which also passes. Demo requests have a window of hours and a reflex of 20 hours, so they fail.

The team's instinct would be to buy a fourth signal. The arithmetic says the cheapest improvement is the one already paid for. Nothing in the demo request needs enriching, scoring or sequencing. It needs a person with a phone and a rota.

The same arithmetic explains why the trucking example is so uncomfortable. Bell argues the pain was public and dated, which means every vendor had the signal. Everyone could see the signal, so seeing it gave nobody an edge. Answering it would have.

What this means for your next quarter

Three actions you can take this week.

  1. Time your own front door. Submit your demo form from a personal address on a Tuesday afternoon and again on a Saturday, and record the hours until a human replies. Compare it with monday's 25 hours, remembering that is one company's account. Write the number on the wall where the pipeline target hangs.
  2. Write a window beside every signal. List your top five signals and put the window next to each, in hours or days. Where nobody can say, mark it unknown. An unknown window is a reason to hold the budget, not to spend it.
  3. Move the money to the shortest window. Pick the signal with the shortest window and give it a named owner and a written response time. Pause spend on the signal with the longest window and the weakest threshold, and review both at four weeks.

Buy fewer signals, and answer the ones you own before the clock does.
