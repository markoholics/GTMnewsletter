Most AI agents can't finish what they start on your site

A long read on why the rush to build go to market for AI agents is outrunning the evidence that agents can act on it, and what to fix this quarter regardless.

By Mohammad H. Rahman  |  19 Nov 2026

By Mohammad H. Rahman

Most of the AI agents sent to complete a task on a company's website still cannot finish it. That detail is missing from almost every piece of advice currently telling founders to get "agent ready," including, remarkably, the one newsletter making the loudest case for it.

The test nobody is leading with

AI Search Leaders ran twenty different AI assistants through the same errand: book a flight. Not describe a flight. Book one. The researchers tracked two things: did the assistant mention the brand at all, and could it actually finish the transaction on the brand's own site (per The GTM Engineering Newsletter, reporting the study).

The split was stark. Agents with their own browser completed 21 of 30 runs. Agents that could only chat, with no browser of their own, completed 2 of 24 (per The GTM Engineering Newsletter). Most of what the industry calls "agent traffic" right now is the second kind: a model answering questions, not a system that can reliably click through a checkout.

One airline won the booking test consistently: Jetstar. The researchers asked the agents what had actually helped, and the answer was mundane: a booking guide kept at one stable address, a documented deep-link format with worked examples, a machine-readable fare table covering bag rules and change fees, a clear statement on whether a price could be held, and a defined handover step where the agent prepares the booking and a human confirms it (per The GTM Engineering Newsletter). None of that required betting on which model wins. It required writing down, once, in a format a programme could parse, what a human already has to explain to every new hire in customer support.

The brand that renamed itself and still came last

A week later, the same newsletter covered a louder story. HubSpot brought Brian Halligan back as chief executive and rebranded the company as "the Customer Harness Company," the idea being that the software wrapped around an AI model (its tools, memory, guardrails) matters more now than the model itself (per The GTM Engineering Newsletter). HubSpot's net new customer acquisition had fallen by half over three quarters, and net revenue retention had slipped from 105% to 101% (per The GTM Engineering Newsletter), so the rebrand reads as an attempt to reframe a slowing growth story around the next buzzword.

Then G2 published its first AI Agent Evaluations: the same fifty tasks, inside a simulated company, scored against what each agent actually did in connected tools rather than what it merely said (per The GTM Engineering Newsletter, citing G2's methodology). Out of 250 possible points, the category leaders Tidio and Retell AI scored 200. HubSpot's own Agent Hub, under the brand that just renamed itself after this exact capability, scored 95 (per The GTM Engineering Newsletter, citing G2).

Sit with that for a second. The company leading the "harness" conversation is also, on the one independent test built to grade harnesses, well behind the category it claims to be redefining. That is not a reason to ignore agents. It is a reason to treat "agent ready" marketing, including your own, as a claim that needs checking rather than a badge you can simply award yourself.

Where the traffic that already converts is coming from

None of this means agents are a non-event. Shopify's president told investors that AI channels sent shoppers to merchant product pages at three times the rate of organic search, and pushed those shoppers into a purchase 64% more often, with AI-referred traffic 2.6 times bigger and AI-referred orders 3.3 times bigger than a year earlier (per The GTM Engineering Newsletter, reporting Shopify's earnings call). Tally, a forms tool, says 43% of its new users now arrive through an AI assistant (per The GTM Engineering Newsletter).

What both of those have in common is being found, not being transacted with directly. A model cited Shopify's merchants and Tally in an answer, and a human followed the link and did the rest. Ross Simmonds, who runs the content agency Foundation, draws the same line from the other direction: Reddit's own shareholder letter reports search referrals down by more than a million a week on average in October, and his advice is to treat that as a warning about your blog too, to track how often AI answers name you, and to get named on the pages AI reads, such as listicles, G2 and comparison pages (per The GTM Engineer Pulse, reporting Simmonds). Being cited, not being booked with, is the part of this shift that is already paying off.

The channel you cannot ask for

Here is the harder truth for anyone planning to build a dedicated AI distribution channel this quarter. The same newsletter that ran the flight-booking test also ranked eight routes to distribution through an MCP server, from lowest to highest impact (per The GTM Engineering Newsletter). At the bottom, shopping and checkout partnerships barely move volume: Walmart converts platform traffic at a third of the rate of its own website. At the top, two routes produce the biggest spikes: being the tool a model chooses to recommend inside a conversation, and being picked as a launch partner by the platform itself. MagicPath says a platform partnership added $500,000 in annual revenue in a week; Stack Overflow's registrations tripled overnight after one (per The GTM Engineering Newsletter).

Those are also the two routes you have no way to request. OpenAI's own words, relayed in the newsletter, are blunt: "Developers cannot request enhanced distribution" (per The GTM Engineering Newsletter, quoting OpenAI). And it can vanish as fast as it arrived. Audioscape's founder, Lukas Campos, described what happened when ChatGPT recommended his product for five days and then stopped: signups ran at 36 times the normal rate, then collapsed, with no change made on his end (per The GTM Engineering Newsletter, citing Campos's own account). "Our listing text was the whole storefront," is how he put it (per The GTM Engineering Newsletter, quoting Campos). GSC Wizard's own daily signups fell by roughly 95% the moment the recommendation stopped (per The GTM Engineering Newsletter).

A channel you cannot request, cannot negotiate for and cannot keep once you have it is not a channel. It is a lottery ticket a platform occasionally hands you, and it should be treated as upside on top of a plan, never as the plan.

What is cheap enough to do anyway

The good news is that most of what actually moves the needle this quarter does not require betting on who wins the model race. Maja Voje's GTM Strategist newsletter, published in partnership with the AI website builder Ploy, describes how Clay's five-person growth team and the analytics company Hex now ship account-specific pages in about two hours rather than days, built from existing account data on industry, tech stack and likely problems (per GTM Strategist, naming Ploy as the tool and sponsor). A new or edited page costing two hours of someone's time, rather than two weeks of a design queue, is worth having whether or not an agent ever reads it, because a human reads it first.

That cheapness is the same lesson as Jetstar's booking guide: a stable pricing page, an honest FAQ, a fare table or its equivalent for your own product, written once in plain, parseable text, helps a human evaluating you at 11pm and an agent checking facts on their behalf equally well. None of it depends on which assistant your buyer happens to be using.

The same logic applies to measurement. MRR Unlocked's founder-led GTM dashboard argues most early teams drown themselves in metrics without first tagging contacts by ICP fit, use case and source (per MRR Unlocked). Their smallest suggestion doubles as the cheapest way to see whether AI referrals are real for your business specifically: keep one open-text CRM field, "how did you hear about us," and save the prospect's own words next to whatever your analytics claims (per MRR Unlocked). A dashboard will tell you "Direct Traffic." A buyer will tell you they asked ChatGPT first. Only one of those is useful.

Why this cannot wait, and why it also should not be rushed

Is doubting the agent-readiness rush just contrarianism for its own sake? Not here, because the doubt comes from the same sources making the opposite case, in the same fortnight: a flight-booking test commissioned by an AI-optimism newsletter shows the category mostly failing, and a harness evaluation from the same newsletter shows the loudest harness brand near the bottom of the table.

Does that mean ignore agents entirely and wait for the category to mature? No, because the citation side of this is already real money: Shopify's merchants and Tally are seeing it today, not in some future quarter, and it cost them nothing beyond being worth citing.

Would a founder actually change a decision because of this? Yes, in a specific way: it argues against spending this quarter's engineering time on a bespoke MCP server chasing a platform partnership you cannot request, and it argues for the far cheaper work (a parseable pricing page, a tracked citation metric, an honest open-text field) that pays off under either outcome, whether agents get much better next year or stay roughly where they are.

Whoever said the early bird catches the worm clearly never watched a chat-only agent fail to finish a flight booking twenty-two times out of twenty-four.

What this means for your next quarter

Three actions a founder can take this week:

  1. Write your own fare table. Pick the one page a buyer or an agent would need facts from most (pricing, plans, integration limits, a comparison) and rewrite it as plain, stable, machine-parseable text: one address, one format, updated in place rather than replaced. Cheap, and useful to a human regardless of what agents can do next year.
  2. Add one line to your dashboard: where were you cited, not just where did you rank. Ask weekly whether AI answers name you in your category, and treat a citation on a listicle, G2 or a comparison page as a result worth protecting, the way Ross Simmonds suggests. It costs an hour, not a build.
  3. Park the bespoke MCP server until you have a reason better than fear of missing out. If a platform invites you into "enhanced distribution," take it. Do not budget headcount this quarter to chase a door you cannot knock on, and remember that whatever it grants you, it can take back overnight.

Put together, here are the four questions worth asking before any agent-readiness project gets a budget line. Evidence: has anyone shown an agent actually completing this task, not just discussing it? Control: who decides whether you get shown, and can you ask for it? Cost: would you build this page anyway, for a human? Reversal: what happens the day the platform changes its mind? A project that fails all four has not earned this quarter's engineering time.
