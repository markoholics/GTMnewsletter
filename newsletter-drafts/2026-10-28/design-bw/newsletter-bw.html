Write the renewal memo before the first deal is signed

A long read on the one page document your buyer's champion will need in ninety days, and why the best operators this week were quietly drafting it on day one.

By Mohammad H. Rahman  |  28 Oct 2026

Every first sale is a vote of faith, and every second sale is an audit.

Kyle Poyar, who writes Growth Unhinged, landed his first newsletter sponsor in under a week (per Growth Unhinged). Renewing that sponsor was another matter: he had to compile reporting, procurement joined the conversation, and the negotiation ran for months. The newsletter had not changed in the meantime. The question the buyer was asking had.

I read this week's GTM newsletters looking for one idea that would change a decision, and four of them, written for different readers, described the same moment from four angles. It is the moment a customer stops believing you and starts checking you. This is a long read, because the fix is a single document, and it only makes sense once you have seen where the pressure comes from.

The week that took a season

Here is how Poyar tells it. Getting a first deal is relatively easy, because lots of brands want to pilot a newsletter placement or promote a launch. The renewal is harder because brands expect quantifiable return or they will not write another cheque. His summary of the pattern is the line I would pin above a desk (per Growth Unhinged).

"You land in brand marketing and expand in growth marketing." Kyle Poyar, Growth Unhinged

The advice he gives writers is just as practical. Report results before you are asked, using a simple sheet with 48 hour and two week figures (per Growth Unhinged). Picture a sponsor's marketing lead three months in. They have to justify a second invoice to a finance colleague who has never read the newsletter, and the only thing they can forward is whatever the writer remembered to send.

A customer who is always on trial

Now take a company where the second sale never stops arriving. Marcus Holm, Chief Revenue Officer at Exa, told the GTMnow podcast that the search company for AI agents has passed $100M in annual recurring revenue, growing roughly tenfold a year for almost three years (per GTMnow). Exa sells on consumption, so there is no large upfront licence and sometimes no renewal date at all. In his phrase, you are always on trial (per GTMnow).

The consequences show up in how Exa pays its people. The comp plan rewards both the first customer and the expansion, with higher rates on contractually committed revenue than on pay as you go (per GTMnow). The salesperson reads usage and tells the customer what they noticed. Holm calls the role a jungle guide (per GTMnow). Nobody waits for a renewal meeting, because the evidence is being produced every day, in the customer's own telemetry.

Few founders will run a consumption business, but many can borrow the discipline. A customer on a flat annual contract is also on trial; the trial simply ends on one date instead of every day. Treat the months before that date as the working period of the renewal, and the date itself stops being a surprise.

An agent that had to earn its next slice

The third case is the most instructive, because it shows evidence being designed rather than discovered. Alex Lindahl, who writes the GTM Engineering newsletter for Clay, describes how Oran Akron rebuilt inbound handling at monday.com. In early 2025 an inbound lead waited about 25 hours on average for a human reply; by the time Akron left, it was under two minutes, with an AI voice agent calling the lead, qualifying them and booking a rep while the prospect was still on the line (per the GTM Engineering newsletter). The agents reportedly handle more than 20,000 leads a month and book around 1,500 discovery calls (per the GTM Engineering newsletter).

What interests me is how it grew. The agent was given a slice of the lead population. Only when its results matched or beat the humans on that slice did it receive more (per the GTM Engineering newsletter). Every call could be reviewed by anyone in the company, and the team ran a daily twenty minute review of automated evaluations. Akron's own estimate was that half his job was change management rather than technology (per the GTM Engineering newsletter).

Read that again as a sales process rather than an engineering one. The customer was the rest of the company, the renewal was every request for a bigger slice, and the evidence had been agreed before the first call was made: what good looks like, expressed as a number.

Four lines of a renewal memo

Put the three cases together and a small habit appears. Before the first deal is signed, write the memo your buyer's champion would send to the person who holds the budget, ninety days from now, asking to continue. Leave the numbers blank. A memo needs only four lines, and I would draft them in this order.

Claim. The one sentence the champion will need to say out loud. "Replies reach us within two minutes" is a claim. "Improved lead handling" is a mood.

Baseline. The figure today, before you touch anything. Akron's 25 hours is a baseline; without it, under two minutes would be a boast.

Slice. The smallest piece of the problem where the claim can be proved or can fail cheaply. One region, one segment, one placement, one creator.

Schedule. When you report, without being asked. Poyar's 48 hours and two weeks is a schedule. So is a daily review.

What a filled in memo looks like

A hypothetical helps here, and I want to be clear that it is invented for illustration. Imagine a Series A analytics company hiring a freelance writer to run sponsored posts in a niche newsletter. Before the contract is signed, the founder and the writer fill in four lines together.

The claim: "Sponsored readers book a demo at a rate our sales lead considers worth paying for." The baseline: the demo requests the company already gets from that audience in a typical month, taken from its own CRM. The slice: one placement in one issue, not a quarter's worth of slots. The schedule: figures sent by the writer at 48 hours and again at two weeks, whether or not anyone asks.

None of that is sophisticated. But when the quarter ends, the founder does not have to reconstruct what they were hoping for, the writer does not have to guess what will be believed, and the finance lead gets a page that already answers the question. If the slice fails, it fails for the price of one placement, and both sides learn that in a fortnight rather than a season.

Why a memo is not a success plan

There is an obvious objection. Sellers already agree success criteria at kickoff, and nobody needs another template. That is true, and I would not pretend otherwise. What I have in mind differs in three ways, and the differences matter more than they sound.

Who actually reads the renewal? Not the person in your kickoff meeting. It is their finance lead, or their chief executive, who have seen none of your work and will give it ninety seconds. What do they have in front of them? Whatever the champion can paste into an email, which is usually a few lines and one screenshot. And what happens if the champion changes jobs? The memo is the only part of you that stays in the building.

So the memo is written in the champion's voice, addressed to their budget holder, and drafted by you at the start with the numbers missing. A success plan describes what you hope will happen. A memo describes what a stranger will need to read. Those documents look similar in a template and behave differently in a meeting.

There is a fair challenge to that, too. Surely a good customer success manager does all of this already? Many do, and the best are relentless about it. But their quarterly review is usually addressed to the person who bought, whereas the memo is addressed to the person who has to approve, and the habit of writing for that second reader is rarer than it should be.

Datarails, covered by GTMnow, adds a useful complication. Its chief revenue officer, Aviv Canaani, gives the VP of Brand no revenue targets and measures engagement from the ideal customer profile instead, having earned that latitude by hitting pipeline targets through paid media first (per GTMnow). He also checks attribution four ways: the CRM, an open question on the website, the sales rep asking live, and mentions on recorded calls, which often disagree (per GTMnow). A memo would not remove that mess. It would name, in advance, which of the four signals the budget holder has agreed to believe.

Where this breaks

Whoever said the first sale is the hardest clearly never had to renew one, but the memo has limits, and I would rather list them than hide them.

First, some value is slow. Poyar makes the point himself: if a brand closes one $100,000 deal after a quarter of ads, the cost per thousand impressions barely matters, and what counts is influence among target buyers (per Growth Unhinged). A memo for a long cycle should therefore claim something earlier than revenue, such as meetings with named accounts, and say plainly that revenue arrives later.

Second, a memo can be gamed. Choose a claim you can hit easily and you will produce a flattering sheet. The defence is to let the champion write the claim, and to start with a slice small enough that failure is visible.

Third, most of my evidence is second hand. Exa's figures come from a podcast summary, monday's from a conversation retold by a newsletter author, and Poyar's from his own experience of roughly a dozen sponsors. None of it is audited. I am proposing a habit that is consistent with these cases, not one that any of them proved.

What this means for your next quarter

Three things a founder can do this week.

  1. Draft a blank renewal memo for your newest customer. Four lines, numbers missing, written as if the champion were sending it to their finance lead. Send it to the champion and ask what they would change. Their edits tell you what their budget holder actually believes.
  2. Shrink your next pilot to a slice. Pick one region, segment or placement, agree the baseline in writing, and make the rest of the rollout conditional on the slice matching its claim. If you are the buyer, ask your vendor, agency or creator for exactly this.
  3. Put a reporting date in the calendar before the kickoff. Choose two moments, an early one within days and a later one within weeks, and send the figures unprompted even when they are mediocre. A dull number delivered on time is worth more than a good one delivered after the question.
