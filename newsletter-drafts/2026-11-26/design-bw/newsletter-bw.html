Before you add another GTM tool, write its quit rule

A long read on why the GTM stack keeps growing, why almost nobody can prove any one piece of it is working, and the one discipline brand marketing already built that tooling hasn't borrowed yet.

By Mohammad H. Rahman  |  26 Nov 2026

By Mohammad H. Rahman

Most GTM teams can tell you exactly how many tools they run and almost nothing about whether any one of them is earning its place. This week's newsletters gave a clean before and after of that gap: one corner of the industry treats a crowded stack as proof of sophistication, and another corner, auditing a single category for accuracy, found that almost no vendor will say how often it is actually right. Between those two stories sits a discipline that already exists, just not where it is needed.

Twenty nine tools and counting

The GTM Engineer School's Pulse newsletter spent its latest issue admiring a stack that keeps growing. Across one cohort's capstone projects, students reached for 28 different tools to solve real client problems (per The GTM Engineer Pulse). A founder quoted in the same issue put the trend in a line worth repeating: "Clay + Claude is where it starts. 29 tools is where it ends up." (per The GTM Engineer Pulse, quoting Jani Vrancsik of growthtoday.co)

Clay itself just opened a global agent into beta that plans and builds across its own modules from a single instruction, reading a company's own knowledge base before it touches a real record (per The GTM Engineer Pulse). The framing throughout is additive. More tools, more modules, more agents wired together, each one a sign the team has arrived.

Nobody in that issue asks the obvious next question: of those 28 tools, which ones would the team notice if they vanished tomorrow? A stack that size usually has an answer for every job and no answer for that one.

The GTM Engineering Newsletter ran the same instinct through a single system this week. Its eight step playbook for building a warm intro program on The Swarm ends not with a finished build but with a list of twelve more plays to add on top, from territory planning to a public Slack channel for intro requests (per GTM Engineering Newsletter). Each addition is sensible on its own. Nobody in the piece asks which of the first eight is actually producing the ten to fifteen intros the playbook promises before the twelfth gets added.

The builders are multiplying too

MKT1's Emily Kramer published 30 separate AI build ideas for marketers this month, covering internal apps, external apps, skills and agents, each one framed as something nobody could have shipped a year ago (per MKT1). Maja Voje's GTM Strategist showed the same instinct from the outbound side: Jordan Crawford's agent chained four tools and several research "hops" through public data to write one personalised email, with every sentence still graded before it was allowed to send (per GTM Strategist).

Both are genuinely useful habits. Neither newsletter asks what happens to build number fourteen once build number three turns out not to move a number anyone cares about. A list of ideas worth building is not the same thing as a list of ideas worth keeping, and only the second list needs a number attached to each entry before the team starts.

The one number almost nobody will publish

GTM Tool Time spent its latest issue trying to answer a narrower version of that question for a single category: intent data, the signals that claim to tell you an account is in market. The newsletter went looking for the number vendors almost never publish: when a tool flags an account as likely to buy, how often is it right?

It found exactly one vendor willing to show the math. Demandbase published a true precision figure for its own account scoring model: in one customer's data, accounts in the top band opened an opportunity within 30 days 31.5% of the time (per GTM Tool Time, citing a Demandbase blog post). Every other vendor in the review answered the accuracy question with scale instead, citing trillions of signals and billions of profiles rather than a measured hit rate.

That is not a coincidence of writing style. It is what a category looks like when accuracy is the thing nobody wants to measure out loud, because the honest number is often underwhelming and the impressive number is always available. A vendor can always report more data. Reporting a correct answer is harder, and rarer.

The newsletter traced the industry's favourite excuse for skipping that measurement back to its source, and the source turns out to be thinner than the pitch decks built on it. The widely quoted claim that roughly 95% of business buyers are never in market at any one time comes from a single researcher who calls it a heuristic, not a measured rule, built on little more than an observation that companies switch banks and law firms every five years (per GTM Tool Time, citing Professor John Dawes). Forrester's own buyer survey cuts the other way entirely: 68% of buyers now start a purchase with a preferred vendor already in mind, and that vendor wins 55% of the time (per GTM Tool Time, citing Forrester). An industry priced in the billions is still running on a heuristic nobody measured and a founding statistic that may already be out of date.

Agents are being handed the same free pass

The same instinct not to measure before scaling shows up again in how GTM teams are deploying agents. The Signal's Brendan Short and Adam Schoenfeld built a matrix for exactly this problem, and their warning is blunt: most of the industry is racing to deploy as many agents as possible, as fast as possible, when the teams getting real results climb a four rung ladder one step at a time (per The Signal).

Their own worked example is deal execution. An agent starts by only answering a rep's questions from the CRM. It graduates to drafting outreach a human still reviews. Only much later, and only for low stakes work, does it act on its own (per The Signal). The same newsletter's sister issue that week carried the arithmetic for why the caution is justified: a workflow that strings together 60 decisions, each individually correct 99% of the time, finishes clean only about 55% of the time (per The GTM Engineer Pulse, relaying Kevin Payne's framing). Chain enough steps and the small, unmeasured errors compound into a result nobody signed off on.

A GTM engineer at Clay described her own version of the same guardrail in practice. Every workflow she ships carries error alerting, and anything irreversible, like flagging a customer account at risk, stops at a human in Slack rather than acting on its own (per The GTM Engineer by Clay). That is the ladder The Signal describes, built by someone who has never read the memo and arrived at the same rule anyway.

What brand marketing already solved

The sharpest version of this discipline this week came from a function nobody expects it from. Cannonball GTM's two part series followed Kelly Megel, who has run brand budgets at Discover, True Value, SharkNinja and Wayfair, through the exact fight that tooling is currently avoiding: how do you prove a spend is working before you keep paying for it?

Her answer has five parts, and none of them are exotic. Name the business result you expect before the money moves. Know your baseline. Write the hypothesis as a sentence a CFO could grade true or false. Hold out a control group, markets or accounts that never see the change, so you can tell a real effect from a coincidence. And write the quit rule before the test starts, not after: this number, by this date, or we stop (per Cannonball GTM).

Her own line on the habit she is defending is the kind of sentence that survives being pinned above a desk: "You know it when you see it in the data." It is aimed at marketers who defend brand spend on instinct, but it reads just as well aimed at a founder about to renew a data vendor on the strength of a sales deck.

Whoever said you can't measure the unmeasurable clearly never watched a fractional CMO turn reach and impressions into a ledger a CFO would sign off on.

The missing mechanism

Here is the point none of this week's newsletters makes, because each one is standing inside its own function. Tooling's problem and brand's solution are the same shape, and nobody has carried the solution across the hall.

A quit rule is not a review. A quarterly tool audit happens after the money is spent and the habit is formed, and by then everyone involved has an incentive to call the tool a success. A quit rule written before the contract is signed has no such incentive, because nobody yet has a reputation riding on the answer. That ordering, before rather than after, is the entire difference between Kelly Megel's ledger and the stack reviews most GTM teams already claim to run.

The same applies to agents. The Signal's ladder tells you how to earn more autonomy. It does not tell you, in writing, what number would make you take autonomy away again once you have granted it. A quit rule does exactly that, for a tool or an agent, and it costs nothing to write except the discomfort of admitting the thing might fail.

None of this requires new software. Clay's agent, the Swarm's warm intro system, the 30 build ideas MKT1 published for marketers this month: every one of them can carry a quit rule without changing a line of code, because the quit rule lives in a document, not a platform. The barrier was never technical. It was that nobody borrowed the habit from the one function already keeping these books.

What this means for your next quarter

Write the quit rule before the signature, not after the renewal. For the next tool or agent on your roadmap, name the number, the date, and the person who checks it, in that order, before the contract is signed. If nobody can write that sentence, that is the answer.

Ask vendors for a hit rate, not a headline. When a vendor pitches you on signal, data or an agent, ask the Demandbase question directly: of the accounts or outcomes you flagged last quarter, how many were actually right? A vendor that cannot answer is telling you something, whatever the rest of the deck says.

Hold out a control group before you roll anything out to everyone. Give one team, one segment or one list the new tool, and leave an equivalent group without it, for one measured period. The gap between the two groups is the only proof you will get that the thing you added was the reason anything changed.
