# Rent your channels. Own the list that travels between them

*A long read on what changed in startup go to market over the last year, and the one column in your plan that nobody on your team owns.*

*By Mohammad H. Rahman*

**Most startups still plan go to market by channel, and the channels have stopped holding still.** Two numbers from this week's newsletters show how fast the ground moved and where the buyer now makes up their mind. Between them sits a part of your plan that every channel owner draws from and nobody looks after.

## The channel you picked is no longer the channel that works

The familiar pattern is to choose a home channel and build a team around it: LinkedIn for account based work, Google for demand, an SDR pod for everything else. Poyar calls the classic B2B demand generation playbook one that is on the decline (per Growth Unhinged), and the numbers below show why a fixed home channel ages badly.

Kyle Poyar, who writes Growth Unhinged, reports that digital advertising jumped from third to first among go to market channels at software companies above $50 million in ARR over the past year (per Growth Unhinged, citing the State of B2B GTM report). Inside paid, the money is moving too. Meta's share of PLG ad budgets rose from 20% last November to 32% as of August (per Growth Unhinged, citing Primer's benchmarks of 379 B2B advertisers). The cost of a thousand LinkedIn impressions is typically 5.3 times Meta's (per Growth Unhinged, citing Primer).

Here is the concrete case. Wispr Flow has 4,300 different ad creatives in the Meta Ad Library (per Growth Unhinged). That is a company treating the channel as a place to run a relentless experiment. Poyar also notes that AI Overviews are squeezing unbranded search inventory on Google, so PLG teams are moving budget to branded terms, including competitors' (per Growth Unhinged).

These figures show the best channel changing inside twelve months, which a plan with a channel hard coded into a team cannot absorb.

## The buyer converts somewhere you do not own

The second change is quieter. Primer's data shows that 59% of confirmed LinkedIn leads between April and August came through native lead forms, which means the buyer never reached the advertiser's website (per Growth Unhinged, citing Primer). Your landing page, the thing your growth team spent a year polishing, was skipped.

Discovery looks similar. GTMnow, the newsletter from GTMfund, summarises a review of 23 studies on AI search: between 75% and 85% of AI citations come from third party and competitor sites, so a brand is 6.5 times more likely to surface through someone else's content than its own, and 56% of AI driven demand arrives as branded search (per GTMnow, citing Noble). Treat that as a single secondary source, since I have not seen the underlying studies.

Emily Kramer of MKT1 points the same way from the creator side. She argues that GTM teams are smaller now and need creators to extend them, because creators bring their own distribution, voice and trust (per MKT1). A lead form, a model's answer and a stranger's post are now where the buyer first meets you, and your site comes afterwards.

## What the sharper operators do about it

They stop planning around the channel and plan around the audience. Poyar's guest, Keith Putnam-Delaney of Primer, describes separating the audience layer from the media buying layer: build the audience from your ideal customer profile, first party data and buying signals, then decide where it is cheapest to reach (per Growth Unhinged). Primer sells audience software, so weigh that, but the logic holds without the product.

The same instinct turns up in a different corner. Jorge B. Macías of The GTM Engineering Company teaches that the account list should be "as narrow and as short as possible" before you check signals against it, and Bogdan Liutic of CR8 adds that the best signals are not on LinkedIn, so you build the list outside and match it in (per GTM Engineer Pulse #48). Brendan Short, who writes The Signal, reports Nooks CEO Dan Lee asking why a marketer decides which ad someone sees when the rep knows the account better (per The Signal, in a sponsored deep dive). Nobody in that chain starts with a channel.

Three writers with different products to sell reached the same unit of planning, which is a list.

## The column nobody owns

Now the point none of those newsletters makes. If the audience is the unit of planning, somebody has to own it, and in most startups nobody does.

Count the lists in your company. Paid keeps one inside each ad platform, as retargeting pools and lookalikes. The SDRs keep another in a sequencer. Content works from a persona deck, and whoever manages creators works from a gut feeling about who is influential. Five lists, five definitions of the ideal customer, each owned by the person whose budget it feeds.

Primer's numbers show the bill. Demand generation money on Google buys leads at roughly $500 each, and only 10% to 20% of them fit the ideal customer profile (per Growth Unhinged, citing Primer). The GTM Engineer Pulse adds a sharper anecdote: Ray Green, who sells managed outbound teams, says the answer to "who calls form fills within minutes?" is often nobody (per GTM Engineer Pulse #48). It is one person's claim with no data behind it, but it describes the same thing from the sales side. Marketing pays for people it cannot describe, and Poyar suspects sales ignores many of them (per Growth Unhinged).

Whoever said you can't have too many channels clearly never tried to reconcile five definitions of the ideal customer on a Monday morning.

Channels have become rentable. You can rent Meta this quarter and LinkedIn the next, rent a creator for a launch, rent an agent to follow up. A list is different because it is the only part of your go to market that gets better the longer you keep it: every reply, every won deal and every churned account teaches it something. That makes it the asset on your balance sheet, and the channels are the leases.

## Why the decision cannot wait for next year

Budgets for the first quarter are being written about now, and the team shape inside them is changing. Bessemer surveyed nearly 175 functional leaders across more than 100 portfolio companies and found that 49% of those companies say they already deliver more without adding headcount (per The GTM Engineering Newsletter, reporting Bessemer). The same issue relays Bessemer's warning against candidates who want to scale account executive headcount before hiring operations roles such as a GTM engineer (per The GTM Engineering Newsletter).

Kim Cram, a recruiter and former chief revenue officer, offers a small case. She put the budget for an extra rep into accelerators for the team she already had, and everyone hit at least 1.5 times quota for three months running (per GTM Engineer Pulse #48). It is one anecdote with no margin data, so I would not copy it. It does show the direction of travel: smaller teams, more rented capacity, and more weight on whoever directs that capacity.

Capacity is for hire. The definition of who you are trying to reach has to be written by someone on your payroll. When the team is small, the owner of that definition is the one person whose absence the other channels will feel at once. That is why it forces a decision this quarter rather than next year.

## Where this view is wrong

Is a list always the right starting point? Not for every motion. MKT1 makes a fair case for using creators to test an audience you have no credibility with yet, and such audiences are by definition not on your list (per MKT1). Keep a slice of budget for exploration, and feed what you learn back into the list.

Does a list slow you down? It can. Primer's advice for Meta is to send the platform at least 50 conversion events a week, and a narrow list may not produce that volume, which pushes you up the funnel to a softer signal (per Growth Unhinged). Is the platform data still useful? Of course. Native forms and lookalikes work, and nothing here says to abandon them.

And is this just account based marketing again? Poyar reports a real resurgence there too (per Growth Unhinged), so the idea is not new. What is new is the requirement that one list serves paid, outbound, content and creators together, owned by one person with the authority to say no.

## The test that tells you whether you rent or own

Try a candid experiment this week. Ask how many working days it would take to move a third of your paid budget to a different platform, with the audience, the conversion signal and the creative ready to go. If the honest answer is weeks because the audience lives inside the platform you are leaving, you are a tenant. Measure it. The number is yours, and it is the one I would put in front of a board.

Put together, the four questions are these. **List:** who exactly are you reaching? **Owner:** who keeps that list honest and can say no to a channel? **Signal:** which conversion do you send back to the platforms? **Exit:** how many working days would it take to leave? A channel that cannot answer all four has not earned next quarter's budget.

## What this means for your next quarter

Three actions a founder can take this week:

1. **Run the switching test.** Write down the number of working days to move 30% of paid spend. Pin it next to your budget. If it is longer than a fortnight, that is your first quarter project.
2. **Name one owner of the list.** Pick someone and give them a short roster of accounts and people, short enough to name from memory in a meeting. Every channel brief for the quarter, including creator briefs, must quote that list or explain why it is exploring outside it.
3. **Send one conversion signal back, and answer it fast.** Choose the lowest funnel event with enough weekly volume, feed it to the platforms you use, and put a named human on every form fill with a measured response time.
